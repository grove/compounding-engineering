This is a deterministic model, not a field result. It compares admission and transfer interactions, not engineering productivity or human effort. Its assumptions are deliberately favorable to reliable measurement and transfers.

One resource, two groups

There are eight capacity units. Every eligible request consumes one unit until fulfillment. Each round ends with verified fulfillment of all admitted requests. Requests arrive in order; denied requests do not wait. Compare four ways of governing access:

Pooled check
Consult a shared check for each request. Accept whenever capacity remains.
Fixed rights
Start with four exclusive units per group. Reject a request when that group's rights are exhausted.
Transfer one
Ask the other group for one unused right when necessary.
Transfer all
Ask for all of the other group's unused rights in one interaction when necessary.

Change the demand

Alternating: 80 opportunities. Shared requests include unsuccessful attempts; discharge interactions excluded.

Fixed rights avoid all shared requests, but under uneven demand they can reject useful work while capacity remains free. Transferring a single right can recreate nearly all the original coordination. Larger transfers work well on the alternating trace, but leave the other group with no rights if shared coordination becomes unavailable.

During the outage, attempted shared admissions and transfers fail. The interactive model counts those attempts.

When would the investment pay?

If N comparable needs currently require a units of coordination effort each, a redesign requiring b per need and F in initial and ongoing investment saves effort only when N × (a − b) exceeds F. For example, 100 changes at four hours each cost 400 hours of coordination. With half an hour remaining per change and 100 hours of investment and upkeep, the redesigned cost is 150 hours. That is an illustrative 2.67-fold reduction in coordination cost, not overall engineering effectiveness.

Compare coordination effort

Existing: 400 hours. Redesigned: 150 hours. Net saving: 250 hours.

Include unsuccessful requests, release interactions, supervision, reallocation, failure recovery and other costs. Only compare like outcomes. A cheap automated pooled check may be preferable to a complex distributed allocation even if it makes more requests.

What the model cannot know

Every promise is recorded, every capacity unit is identical, rights are exclusive, transfers are instantaneous when available and fulfillment is known. Real customers may rely on an experimental service without anyone recording it. Specialists may become unavailable. Retiring a service stops new commitments but does not discharge existing ones. The model cannot establish that any of these assumptions hold.

The research note (review draft) explains the initial comparison, subsequent sensitivity and limitations. The underlying technical research concerns coordination and invariants in distributed databases. Applying it to organizations remains a hypothesis.

Try the proposed experiment on one actual constraint. Compare equivalent customer outcomes at equivalent risk, then examine whether the investment helps develop a distinct later capability.

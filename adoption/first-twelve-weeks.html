The first twelve weeks

Start transforming one workflow. Change how the work happens, then check whether the whole result improves.

Start here Investing Worksheet Organizational patterns The first twelve weeks

Start transforming one workflow

Ask an engineering and product lead to pick one frequent class of valuable outcomes. In the hypothetical migration example, that is database changes reaching production with the required service behavior. Include investigation, decisions, setup, implementation, release and follow-up. The existing sequence of tickets and meetings is something to question.

Choose whether to remove work, automate a routine path or redesign a dependency that makes the work necessary. Work that appears twice a year needs a different observation window. Use the existing twelve-week field check as the measurement basis; this playbook adds the organizational decisions.

Agree on a real quality bar with the appropriate domain and risk owners. Do not substitute a simulated check for required production safeguards. The dates below are a suggested steering horizon, not deadlines to force a second investment or cross-team rollout.

Weeks 1–2: Find the limiting condition

Review a few recent comparable changes, including awkward and unsuccessful ones. Trace active effort, waits, rework, acceptance and dependencies. Mark each place where a person starts, coordinates, approves or rescues the procedure, and ask what purpose it serves. Speak with people upstream and downstream. Choose an outcome and a suspected constraint; compare removing the chore, changing the design, automating it and using existing capacity better.

Engineering and product leads agree on a bounded experiment and the work it displaces. Name a maintainer, a review trigger and a stop condition using the investment note. If the diagnosis points to a missing decision instead of a missing tool, make that decision first.

Try AI on the diagnosis

If finding and comparing the cases is taking too long, try AI on that part of the work. For example, give it a small set of migration reviews the participants can access. Ask it to distinguish waiting for technical evidence from waiting for a decision, cite each case and include cases that resist the grouping. An engineer checks the source material before choosing an explanation to test.

Agree the time and review cap first. Write down a credible alternative and what result would favor it. Try the smallest check that separates them: a prepared lock scenario might remove setup work, but it cannot decide how much downtime a customer can accept. Stop if the cases do not support a common problem or the investigation reaches its cap.

Keep the question, evidence and decision in the issue already open. Record source preparation, model use, review effort and misleading suggestions. The later observation is whether this diagnosis led to a useful change. This trial alone cannot establish that AI improved the diagnosis or saved time; that needs a comparison.

Weeks 3–4: Change how the work happens

Try the smallest change that could relieve the constraint. For migrations, that might mean independent environments and a reliable way for billing to assess an eligible change without orders' specialist. If the dependency is unnecessary, try removing it. Producing a faster review summary is a smaller claim and should be evaluated as such.

For a routine dependency workflow, establish what starts a run, how it gets an isolated environment, what evidence permits it to finish, and what brings an exception to a person. Exercise the whole path, including cleanup and follow-up. Automating the work of engineering gives a worked hypothetical example.

Establish the expected result from the agreed behavior, independently of the implementation. Include a failure case and a nearby case where the suspected problem is absent; an agent's fix and its tests can share the same mistake. Record build effort, supervision and deferred commitments. Let someone other than the author use or operate the workflow.

If the initial bet fails its quality bar or runs out of budget, stop and decide whether to repair, reduce scope or remove it. Do not extend the cap just to finish the experiment.

Weeks 5–8: Watch later work

Record every eligible change in the small selected class where practical, or agree an unbiased sampling rule in advance. Include abandoned attempts, workarounds, maintenance, missing checks, specialist support and later corrections. Use ordinary work records; avoid creating another tracking system.

Ask whether the initial constraint has moved. Faster review may simply leave more migrations waiting for release approval. Did the old handoff disappear, or does someone now operate a different queue? Measure accepted outcomes and human attention across the workflow at the same or stronger quality bar. Compare like work; record changes in demand, difficulty, staffing and tooling. Do not convert directional observations into individual performance scores.

If the investigation used AI, revisit its explanation as well as the tool. Did a later migration behave as predicted? If you repeat the analysis, check whether it keeps proposing rejected changes. Narrow or stop it when reviewing its output costs more than the problem warrants.

Weeks 9–10: Test the next connection, if one exists

Follow the new constraint before choosing another investment. If another team has a real eligible migration, allocate receiver time and let its engineer adapt and interpret the workflow. If a recovery rehearsal is now worthwhile, see whether the earlier environments and cases reduce setup or improve verification. These are different questions: a capability can travel without compounding, and it can compound without crossing teams.

A software farm can investigate the factory's repeated interventions and propose a change to its machinery. For example, retained update failures might help build a diagnostic that collects the missing evidence automatically. An isolated update environment might make a runtime-upgrade experiment affordable. Compare the new work required with what the earlier capability contributed; do not infer compounding from another scheduled job.

If no useful later investment or receiving task arrives, skip this step. Never invent extra infrastructure to complete the playbook.

Weeks 11–12: Make a decision

Use the Investment retrospective. Read individual change notes before comparing averages. Invite the people who paid for upkeep and the people who benefited. A conclusion can be that the bet was useful, inconclusive, unnecessary or damaging.

Suppose the migration workflow helps on two changes but billing still needs support and no later capability is built. The team may keep a simpler version if the observed benefits justify the expected upkeep. It has not established compounding or an organization-wide gain. If a shared service boundary still forces specialist coordination, investigate changing that boundary. If the wait is for a business decision, improve how that decision gets made. If the workflow is unreliable, withdraw it with an appropriate safe alternative.

Decide where genuinely recovered capacity goes. Some may address customer needs; some may fund the next constraint or preserve room for investigation. Make the allocation explicit so improving the engineering system can continue. No fixed reinvestment percentage is required.

Twelve weeks of ordinary work rarely provide a credible causal comparison and may be too short to observe a later capability. Report this limitation; do not extrapolate to tenfold effectiveness. A negative or inconclusive result is useful for steering the next investment. The proposed research comparisons separate the question of whether retained work helps build a later capability from whether AI helps the investigation itself.

Before asking another team to adopt it

Check that the receiving team's problem is real, that it has time and authority to try the capability, and that support and ownership are funded. Keep the scope and exception path visible. If the same friction appears elsewhere, use the organizational patterns to select another bounded experiment. If it does not, keep the useful local improvement and return to delivery.

What this playbook is not: a completed field study, a maturity assessment, a promise of savings or a requirement to produce artifacts. It is a proposed way for a team to learn from an investment while doing its normal work.

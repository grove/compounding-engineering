Enough detail to make a decision

Use this for a bet that needs a planning conversation. A small fix inside an ordinary task can stay small. The questions are prompts, so leave out what you do not need and copy the note into the issue you already use.

The distant tenfold ambition gives this experiment a purpose: learn how to change the work and make later capability development easier.

Follow the work from the initial need to a useful result. Which steps can disappear? Which can run automatically? What would a different interface, decision rule or division of work make possible? Automating the work of engineering develops these questions beyond the time spent writing code.

Read a worked example

This hypothetical product team waits for a platform engineer to prepare and later remove a preview environment for each experiment. The proposed change removes that request queue: eligible branches receive an isolated environment automatically, with a cost limit and an expiry. Existing deployment templates and synthetic data provide a starting point.

The team sets an illustrative two-day build cap, half a day for adoption and a review after five eligible experiments or twelve weeks. It predicts that removing the environment queue will bring product decisions forward. It compares that prediction with observations, including human interventions, failed provisioning, cleanup and total cost. If environments arrive sooner but decisions do not, the team revisits its explanation of the delay.

Recovered platform time goes toward the next constraint. Keep the useful evidence and understanding of which experiments fit the workflow, alongside the provisioning and cleanup machinery. A later team might use them to build independent agent investigations, after checking the assumptions still apply. That is a separate investment to evaluate; storing a lesson or reusing a template does not establish compounding.

The proposed investment

Name who benefits, what they can accomplish and the conditions it covers. Describe the result beyond the automated task.

Follow a recent case through waiting, decisions, execution and follow-up. Name another plausible cause and any step or handoff that could disappear.

Include people's understanding and judgment, agreed decisions, interfaces, automation and access. What would need adapting before people or agents could use them independently?

Specify what you will remove, redesign or automate in one bounded experiment. What starts the work, what lets it finish, and which exceptions still need a person?

What is its main purpose?

State a prediction and an observation that could disprove it. Compare outcomes and surprises, then retain or revise the explanation. Measure time, quality, total human attention and economic cost, including failed attempts, upkeep and work shifted to others.

Name a later capability, the understanding and tools it could inherit, and how recovered capacity could help build it. Who must be able to apply the learning, and what changed assumptions would require revisiting it? An immediate return can still be enough.

Name the owner, users and authority to change the workflow. Include build, adoption, oversight, compute, upkeep and the work displaced.

Set a review trigger and a date in case the expected work never arrives. Say what would justify stopping.

Come back after the workflow runs

Add observations and surprises beside the prediction. Was the outcome achieved, and does the explanation of the constraint still hold? Retain or revise it before deciding what to keep, simplify, repair or retire. Compounding learning follows this investigation through to later use.

Keep the evidence, conditions and understanding needed to apply the lesson. Let another person or team try it on real work, and name who will revisit it when assumptions change. If a later capability is built, record what it inherited, what had to be learned or added, and where recovered capacity went. That is where the compounding claim can be tested. A stored explanation alone does not show that anyone has learned.

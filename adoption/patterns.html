When the constraint is organizational

The technical pattern catalogue covers capabilities teams can build and combine. These six companion patterns address who can redesign the work, change a dependency and fund what follows. They are proposed practices, not validated organizational interventions. Pick the problem you have; adopting all six is not a goal.

Each uses the hypothetical orders and billing migration story from the adoption guide.

Protected investment

Problem. Every deadline is a reason to defer the improvement that would make the next deadline easier to meet.

Try. Engineering and product leads agree a capacity cap, displaced work, review date and authority to change the workflow. Orders gets two days to prepare the lock check; billing gets time to try independent migration assessment. The agreement includes when the recurring specialist handoff can end. A genuine incident may interrupt the bet, but the next planning conversation explicitly restores, changes or cancels it.

Look for. The investment actually happens without hidden overtime; subsequent eligible work meets the agreed outcome and quality bar with fewer recurring interventions. Count the work deferred, transition effort and coordination cost.

Stop or adapt. If the need disappears or the experiment fails, release the commitment. Do not protect an arbitrary percentage or reserve a permanent improvement backlog that nobody can challenge.

Capability transfer

Problem. One team fixes a recurring failure; the next team still needs the original author to avoid it.

Try. Transfer the question, applicability conditions, runnable capability and expected result to a named receiver with time to try it. Billing tests the lock behavior of its own database and traffic assumptions, rather than copying orders' migration patch. Give the receiver the access and decision rights needed to act independently. Observe the next actual outcome, including a justified decision not to use the check.

Prepare the receiving team too. Can its engineer explain a lock queue, obtain the relevant access and recognize a case outside the check's scope? Pair on the missing part, then let the receiver handle another case. Cohen and Levinthal's absorptive capacity gives a reason for this investment: prior knowledge affects whether new knowledge can be understood and applied. Count that learning time in the transfer cost.

AI can help find likely receivers by looking for the relevant database and migration tools in repositories it is allowed to read. A matching tool is a reason to ask a question. Billing still needs to test its own lock behavior, traffic and release conditions. Keep those differences with the check so the next receiver can judge the fit.

Look for. Independent discovery, correct interpretation and a changed decision where appropriate. Does the original author's queue actually shrink, or has it become a support queue? Record adaptation effort and unanswered questions. A page view or an install is not a transfer.

Stop or adapt. Local context may make the check irrelevant. Repair a confusing handoff only when a real receiver needs it. Do not require universal reuse. See Verified retrieval.

Shared-benefit funding

Problem. Orders pays for maintaining a check that principally benefits billing, so either its manager declines the work or support gets done invisibly.

Try. Bring the decision to the leader responsible for both outcomes. Compare a shared service with a portable capability or a design that removes the cross-team dependency. Agree a contribution, support owner, upkeep cap and receiver time. Make the displaced work visible on both plans. Include an exit arrangement if the shared need disappears.

Look for. Benefit to receiving teams without deterioration in the originating team's commitments or hidden support load. Track who bears the ongoing costs, not just aggregate savings.

Stop or adapt. If coordination costs exceed the expected benefit, let teams keep local checks instead. Do not centralize merely to obtain funding; do not make a team absorb mandatory platform migrations without capacity to do the work.

Deliberate reinvestment

Problem. A useful capability reduces recurring work and the released capacity immediately disappears into new commitments.

Try. Verify the saving and where it lands. The team that no longer fields specialist calls can allocate some attention to the next constraint: automating migration preparation, separating a shared environment or investigating whether a service is still needed. Customer work, mentoring and protective slack also need capacity. Record the allocation with the next work owner.

Look for. Usable capacity rather than theoretical minutes, net of upkeep and new work. If old cases or isolated environments help build the next workflow, keep evidence of what transferred and what still had to be done. Count the capacity once. Reinvestment funds the next attempt; evidence that earlier capabilities helped build it supports the compounding claim.

Stop or adapt. No verified saving means no dividend to allocate. A risk-reduction investment may be worthwhile anyway. Do not impose reinvestment quotas or equate the dividend with headcount that can be removed.

Earned standardization

Problem. A local success becomes a company-wide rule before anyone knows whether it fits another team's work.

Try. Start with a portable check. Try it in the contexts where the risk actually applies. Compare a copied command, shared library and central service, including support, versioning and migration costs. If a common standard is justified, document scope, reason, owner, exception path and review trigger.

Look for. Independent use and better decisions across relevant contexts without a new central queue. Check what teams bypass and why. Invite dissent from people paying the costs.

Stop or adapt. Keep local variants if the assumptions diverge. Optional operational conventions may be retired after review; legal, security and safety requirements do not expire automatically. Some controls need central enforcement from the outset; retain their required authority and verify their effectiveness rather than waiting for voluntary adoption.

Judgment stewardship

Problem. A check or agent handles routine work, but fewer people can explain a failure, recognize its limits or improve it.

Try. Give engineers practice in setting outcomes, judging evidence, resolving exceptions and improving the machinery. On an ordinary migration, ask a receiver to predict the lock and queue behavior, run the check, explain a surprise and decide what the result does not establish. Use a safe rehearsal for rare failures. Rotate ownership with mentoring and real time. Expand delegation when independent evidence meets the required bar; preserve escalation and the ability to suspend automation.

Occasionally rehearse without the usual expert or service. The question is what the team can still diagnose and recover, and where it needs a fallback. Preserve the understanding or alternative route that the rehearsal shows is necessary. Keeping every old process is not a substitute for making that choice.

An agent can prepare a variation on a retained case, such as a migration against an empty table instead of a busy one. Have an experienced engineer check the exercise and its expected result. Let the learner predict before seeing the explanation, then change a condition to see whether the reasoning holds. A fluent answer to the original case is weak evidence of understanding.

Look for. More than one person can reason through unfamiliar cases and make a sound decision. Count training and supervision effort alongside automation benefits.

Stop or adapt. Do not turn every ticket into an examination, or remove the specialist from high-risk work before the receiver is ready. If understanding declines, narrow delegation and repair the learning opportunity. See How engineers become engineers and Earned delegation.

Check the combined burden

Six individually defensible interventions can still create six meetings, competing policies and unwanted support work. Use the existing retrospective to ask what work disappeared, which outcome improved and where human attention now goes. Remove what does not earn its keep. Adoption itself is an investment.

Apply the same review to an AI-assisted learning workflow. Count the cases it missed, false leads it produced, time spent checking its suggestions and whether a useful result reached the next task. If it repeatedly finds the same unresolved issue, decide who can act on it before producing another summary. The Investment retrospective can cover the investigation method as well as the capability it produced.

Transformation experiments

What could engineering become?

Choose a consequential constraint. Test a different way to organize the work. Find out what becomes possible, what remains difficult and whether the next transformation gets easier.

Begin with the outcome, not the procedure

Suppose software changes can be generated almost instantly, but reaching a decision, obtaining an environment, establishing correctness or getting a release accepted still takes days. Making the implementation step faster may barely change the result.

Imagine organizing this workflow from scratch with today's capabilities. Which activities would you no longer create? Which constraints would still hold? Who or what needs to make each decision, and what evidence would make delegated decisions trustworthy?

Use the investment worksheet to select a bounded first experiment. Compare the current workflow, eliminating the work, redesigning it and automating what remains. Choose by expected effect on useful outcomes, rather than ease of building an AI tool.

These are candidate experiments, not case studies. No results or savings are claimed below. Start with work that matters to your organization, and adapt the tests to the risks and constraints of that work.

Five experiments worth considering

1. Dependency maintenance without a standing queue

Constraint: routine upgrades repeatedly interrupt teams or accumulate until they become urgent. Redesign: let a factory discover eligible updates, prepare and verify changes in isolated environments, deliver within policy and route uncertain cases to an owner. Keep high-risk changes outside automatic release until there is evidence and authority to expand it.

Test: compare time to a safely deployed update, overdue exposure, manual touches, escaped faults and total run and upkeep cost. Can the resulting environment and verification capabilities handle another class of maintenance, such as a runtime migration?

2. Investigate continuously, change selectively

Constraint: recurring faults, expensive code paths or obsolete features are rarely investigated unless they become emergencies. Redesign: run a farm with a bounded budget and a portfolio of consequential questions. Require supporting evidence and counterexamples, and let findings propose a change, a further experiment or no action.

Test: count accepted findings that change decisions, false leads, review effort, avoided work and effects on reliability or cost. Does one investigation develop a diagnostic method that makes a different investigation cheaper?

3. Eliminate a cross-team dependency

Constraint: valuable changes wait for a shared specialist, environment, approval or synchronized release. Redesign: examine actual waiting cases. Try independently verifiable contracts, clearer decision rights or on-demand environments; remove the handoff if its purpose can be met without it.

Test: measure request-to-outcome time, the proportion of eligible work completed independently, failures at the boundary and support costs. Does the new boundary make an additional team or class of work independent?

4. Make a previously impractical opportunity feasible

Constraint: a customer need or product experiment is not worth pursuing under current implementation and validation costs. Redesign: combine existing domain knowledge, prototypes, isolated environments and evidence machinery to investigate the opportunity without committing to a full build. Use AI where it expands exploration, not just output.

Test: compare time and cost to a defensible product decision, including abandoned alternatives. If the outcome is promising, verify actual user value. Did reusable capabilities lower the cost of a subsequent opportunity? A decision not to build can be valuable.

5. Let the engineering system expose its own constraints

Constraint: repeated interventions in delivery are treated as individual exceptions, rather than clues to improve the system. Redesign: collect permitted evidence about waits, retries, manual interventions and successful runs. Use a farm to test explanations and suggest changes to the factory. Implement a selected improvement through the factory's normal verification and release path.

Test: measure investigation cost, useful changes to the delivery system, whether recurring interventions really disappear, and whether the new capability helps investigate or remove another constraint. Keep a human owner for priorities, permission changes, budgets and stopping the experiment.

These possibilities are related to the essays on automating engineering, continuous cultivation and tenfold effectiveness.

Let farms and factories improve one another

  1. Direct: people set valuable outcomes, constraints, priorities, risk boundaries and investment budgets.
  2. Investigate: a farm uses operational observations and domain understanding to test explanations, challenge assumptions and discover alternatives.
  3. Decide: an accountable owner decides what to remove, redesign, automate or leave alone.
  4. Deliver: a factory implements, verifies and delivers the selected change within delegated authority; uncertainty goes to an appropriate decision-maker.
  5. Learn: compare predicted with observed outcomes, including useful surprises, errors and cost. Develop shared judgment and retain capabilities that another person or workflow can use.
  6. Reinvest: use the improved system to pursue a more valuable outcome or the next limiting constraint. Test whether the earlier investment made this advance easier.

This is a feedback loop, not an organizational chart. One team may operate both roles. Do not automate the selection of priorities or expansion of permissions merely because a workflow can execute unattended. Stop or narrow an investment that cannot establish its benefit.

Record what actually happened

Use the twelve-week playbook to follow the experiment through later work. Agree on the outcome and quality bar before changing the workflow. Observe representative eligible work before and after, or use a credible comparison where possible. Include failed attempts, exceptions, changes in work mix and other plausible explanations.

Follow useful outcomes per unit of total cost alongside elapsed time, human attention and quality. Count compute, tools, setup, supervision, rework and maintenance. Do not multiply unrelated speedups into a claim of 10×. Where outcomes cannot be reduced to a defensible common unit, report several measures and the trade-offs instead of a single ratio.

Separate a plausible route from a demonstrated one

Proposed
A hypothesis and plan; not evidence of an improvement.
Observed
Results from a bounded intervention, with context and limitations. An improvement in one workflow is not a team-wide transformation.
Repeated or transferred
Useful results on later eligible work or in another context, with adaptation and upkeep accounted for. This can demonstrate sustained or recurring benefit.
Compounding connection
Evidence that an earlier investment helped create a later capability more effectively. Reuse by itself is not enough. A connection may fail to transfer or make a later investment more expensive.
System-level effectiveness
Outcomes across the relevant value stream over time, including the costs of all the enabling investments and required quality. Establish this separately from individual experiments.

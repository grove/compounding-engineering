Investing in engineering capital

Decide who can invest, who pays, and what to do with the capacity an investment actually releases.

Give the investment a place in planning

The orders team proposes spending two days turning its lock investigation into a check another engineer can use. Billing would gain independence, but orders would lose two days of feature capacity. Who gets to say yes?

Product work and capability work are both investments in future outcomes. Some work instead preserves a guarantee or reduces an exposure the business is unwilling to accept. Name which outcome matters; do not dress every maintenance task up as a speed gain.

Before building anything, compare the bet with doing nothing, eliminating the underlying chore, using an existing capability, or settling a missing product decision. The investment selection questions help with this. Look at frequency, severity, the suspected constraint, cost, alternatives, likely useful life and what a later capability might inherit.

If the cause is uncertain, fund a short investigation first. AI may help collect the cases and test explanations, but its recommendation is not an approved investment. Agree who will check the evidence, how much review time is available and what finding would justify stopping. The learning exercise shows how to keep that work small.

Leave room for more than today's work

A quicker migration review is useful if migrations are what need to happen. It tells you little about whether a different storage design could remove much of that work. Consider three purposes when choosing an investment:

Improve current work
Make a known job more dependable or less expensive. The lock check belongs here. Compare later migrations under the same acceptance standard.
Explore a different approach
Resolve an uncertainty that could change what you choose to do. Try a small alternative design against the difficult cases before funding a migration. A well-supported decision to stop is a useful result.
Preserve the ability to respond
Keep an important option or understanding available. Rehearse a recovery without the usual specialist, or verify that the team can replace a dependency. Name the conditions this prepares you for and what it costs.

The distinction between refining familiar work and exploring new possibilities comes from James March's work on organizational learning. Preservation is an additional planning prompt used here. These are purposes, not fixed budget percentages; one investment may serve several.

At a planning review, revisit a worthwhile initiative previously rejected as too expensive or risky. Ask whether new capabilities have changed that judgment, keeping the budget, quality and acceptable risk visible. What becomes possible? develops this exercise.

Match authority to the commitment

Inside a task
An engineer can retain a small check or repair an existing tool within the ordinary task budget. No separate pitch is needed.
Across a team's work
The engineering and product leads agree on a bounded experiment, the displaced commitment and an owner. If a decision needs a risk or domain owner, get that agreement too.
Across teams
A leader responsible for the benefiting teams settles the allocation of build, adoption and maintenance capacity. The receiving team agrees to try it; do not assign invisible support work to the originating team.
Across the organization
The accountable leader compares the investment against other system constraints and funds its lifecycle, including exit or migration. A local success is not sufficient authority to impose a shared standard.

Agree locally on what counts as a small bet. There is no universal percentage to protect. A heavily constrained team may need a larger temporary allocation; a healthy team may need little. If urgent work interrupts the investment, name the reason, the work being deferred and the next decision date. Do not recover the time through overtime.

A hypothetical migration investment

In this example, billing's routine migration reviews repeatedly wait for orders' specialist. First check whether specialist review is actually holding up accepted changes, rather than release approval. The bet is a two-day build cap plus separately agreed receiver time: a reproducible check of lock behavior and connection queuing on representative synthetic cases. Product and engineering leads agree which work moves. The orders team owns the check during the experiment; billing names a receiver and its lead allocates adaptation time. Agree an upkeep cap before retaining it.

The expectation is that billing can judge eligible migrations without the specialist, using the same reviewed acceptance standard. The cases might later supply inputs for a recovery rehearsal. That is a hypothesis, not a saving. Observe the next three eligible migrations or review at twelve weeks, whichever comes first; this is a review trigger, not statistical proof. Stop if the cases mislead, upkeep exceeds the agreed cap, or the review no longer limits useful work. Preserve the required release and database safeguards either way.

Do not spend the same hour twice

If billing no longer needs the specialist, find out whose usable capacity changed. A saved ten-minute interruption in orders does not automatically release ten minutes on billing's delivery plan. Count support, exception handling, adoption, verification and upkeep. If work simply moves into a different queue, the system may not deliver more.

Make an explicit allocation decision in ordinary planning. The available choices include customer work, investment at the new constraint, risk reduction and slack to absorb variability or learn. Reinvesting some capacity can be worthwhile, but it is not obligatory and there is no universal split. Do not promise an immediate headcount or throughput gain from estimated savings.

Compounding hypothesis: the retained lock scenarios reduce the effort or improve the quality of building a new recovery rehearsal. Track which scenarios transfer, the new work and the adaptation cost; distinguish observations from estimates of starting over. A recurring saving alone does not establish that effect.

Review the investment against its remaining benefit, not the effort already sunk into it. If it continues to protect a necessary guarantee, retain or replace it appropriately even if the original delivery constraint has moved. Remove obsolete optional checks only after identifying their consumers; mandatory controls need their own risk-owner review and safe replacement or retirement.

Engineer the engineering system

Suppose a team can prepare changes quickly, but every experiment waits for someone to configure a shared environment. Engineers coordinate access, repair state left by the previous experiment and explain the setup to the next person. Faster implementation leaves that system largely intact.

The team could build a service that provisions isolated environments from a declared configuration and retires them afterward. Investigations could then arrange their own infrastructure. A procedure performed on request becomes a capability available whenever work needs it, and the request queue can disappear for eligible work.

This is a hypothetical redesign. It succeeds only if useful investigations finish sooner or cost less at the required quality, including the effort of operating the service. What the team learns from that change, and what the new capability enables next, matter to its longer ambition.

Our ambition is tenfold effectiveness at delivering valuable software outcomes. The environment example raises a question about how to pursue it: could one redesign make the next redesign easier? A service might save setup work, teach us which dependencies matter, and make a different kind of investigation affordable. Those are separate claims to examine.

The paths guide explores possible sequences. This page sets out the distinctions needed to judge them.

Treat the workflow as a design choice

Ask how we would organize the work with the capabilities available now. Which steps accomplish a necessary purpose? Which exist because people must remember to start a job, carry context between tools or wait for a specialist? The answer may change task boundaries, architecture, decision rights and the point at which work begins.

Acceleration
An agent helps an engineer configure the environment faster. The existing workflow takes less effort.
Automation
A service provisions the environment when eligible work arrives. The repeated procedure no longer needs someone to perform it.
Transformation
Independent investigations provision their own environments, compare alternatives and preserve their results. The organization can investigate differently because a coordination dependency has gone.

These changes can overlap. A useful automation may transform a workflow; a simpler architecture may transform it without adding an agent. Each recurring demand on human attention is a candidate for elimination, redesign or automation. The resulting system must still accomplish the purpose that matters.

Follow the whole route from need to outcome. Discovery may require better access to customer evidence. A shared business rule may need a clear owner. An interface change may let teams release independently. A diagnostic procedure may become part of responding to an incident. Testing is one enabling capability within this larger design space.

The Theory of Constraints discussion gives investment a direction: identify what limits a chosen outcome, use existing capacity better, align surrounding work with it, change the constraint where necessary, and then look again. A procedure that becomes much faster can still leave the outcome unchanged.

The tenfold ambition gives this search a direction. A bounded redesign of real work can reveal what becomes possible, what the team needs to learn and which investment should follow. Keep the destination ambitious while allowing the route to change with the evidence.

Software farms and software factories

A software farm organizes continuing investigation and cultivation. It starts with a consequential question: whether a guarantee holds, why a workflow repeatedly stalls, whether a simpler design would suffice or whether the rationale for an old component still applies. Its useful output can be evidence, an explanation or a reason to leave the system alone.

A software factory organizes the implementation and delivery of changes. It starts with work to be done and brings together environments, tools, verification and release mechanisms. Automating the work of engineering describes how a recurring class of work can proceed within agreed authority, returning exceptions that require a decision.

The roles reinforce each other. A farm can find work worth sending to the factory. The factory's failures, delays and operating results can supply questions for the farm. Both can improve the engineering machinery itself: removing a setup dependency, diagnosing an ambiguous failure or simplifying a tool that has become expensive to maintain.

They need not be separate teams or products, and they are not a mandatory sequence. The same people and tools can serve both. Start where the constraint matters. An organization with adequate delivery capacity may need better discovery; one with a growing queue of useful findings may need to improve how those findings become decisions and delivered changes.

People remain accountable for goals, priorities and the authority delegated to the system. Routine work can proceed without requiring a new human instruction at every step, while consequential changes to purpose, acceptance rules or authority remain explicit decisions. Greater autonomy is useful when it improves outcomes and the attention required to achieve them.

Owning a tool is not enough

Engineering capital is what an organization can draw on: people's understanding, executable tools, recorded knowledge, useful boundaries and ways of working together. A capability is something the organization can reliably accomplish through its people and systems under stated conditions.

A provisioning tool is capital. Being able to run an isolated investigation without a setup handoff is a capability. The difference includes access, meaningful inputs, cleanup, cost control and a way to recognize when the environment is unsuitable. A repository full of tools can coexist with a team that still needs the original author for every attempt.

Name a capability as work the system can do: “an engineer or agent can start a representative performance experiment, obtain interpretable results and retire its environment within the agreed budget.” State the limits. The environment might support performance comparisons while leaving production failover outside its scope.

This keeps investment attached to its purpose. The result we want is useful work that becomes feasible or requires less intervention and cost. The asset is one means of getting there.

Develop the ability to act independently

Engineering capital can include an agreed business contract, credible assurance and established decision rights, not just software and recorded knowledge. Together they may let a team carry out a class of changes without repeatedly obtaining the same information or permission. The capability is demonstrated when independent actions produce acceptable collective outcomes.

There is an important limit: independently valid changes can compete for recovery capacity or create conflicting promises. A low-cost shared check may be the simplest answer. In a measurable, exclusively allocatable resource, standing rights can sometimes remove that transaction from each change. Establishing, observing and reallocating rights costs effort; hidden reliance and changes in available capacity can invalidate them.

Investigate whether learning reveals the recurring dependency, whether simplification removes it, and whether the resulting boundary makes autonomy and parallel work economical. Recurring savings establish a return. Compounding requires evidence that the capability helps develop a distinct later capability, perhaps migration or withdrawal of a service, after adaptation and upkeep.

The integration case follows this difficulty into operation; the synthetic model illustrates both the opportunity and its limits.

What, exactly, is compounding?

Several useful things can happen to the same retained work. They deserve different names because they require different evidence.

Accumulation
The environment configuration and provisioning command survive in the repository. We have kept something.
A recurring return
The service removes setup effort from the next eligible investigation. It has helped again.
Complementarity
Isolated environments become more useful with representative workloads and interpretable telemetry. The investments reinforce one another.
Composition
The team combines provisioning, workloads and comparison tools into a repeatable performance investigation.
Compounding
The earlier investments reduce the cost or improve the quality of developing that new investigation capability, after adaptation and upkeep are counted.

Complementarity and composition can enable compounding. Neither establishes it on its own. Repeated use can justify a tool even when no further capability follows.

Learning connects these investments. In the hypothetical environment example, an engineer's understanding becomes a declared configuration. Experiments clarify which dependencies matter. That evidence supports a simpler boundary, which makes independent delivery possible. Other engineers can learn from those cases and design the next investigation with less help. Knowledge moves between judgment, executable tools, recorded reasoning and architecture.

Each connection needs examination. A copied configuration may carry the wrong assumption; a retained explanation may no longer fit. The next person or agent should be able to apply the understanding and recognize its limits. Revise or retire what misleads. Compounding learning follows this work from an explanation to later use.

Decide where recovered capacity will go. Some can deliver customer value; some can fund investigation of the next constraint. Reinvestment gives the transformation a way to continue. Record separately whether the earlier capabilities actually made that next investment easier. Savings and a growing collection of tools do not establish this on their own.

Bruce Kogut and Udo Zander's work on combinative capabilities describes how firms develop skills by recombining existing capabilities, including the ways people cooperate. Martin Weitzman's recombinant growth model examines combinations of ideas; Milgrom and Roberts' work on complementarities explains why investments can be more valuable together. These are foundations for the argument, not validation of this particular engineering practice.

Try a small composition before drawing an organization-wide capability map. Choose a worthwhile task that is currently difficult, identify two or three things you already know how to do, and test whether connecting them helps. The Capability composition pattern walks through that decision.

Improve the way you improve

Delivering a useful change does engineering work. Building a factory that can deliver that class of change improves how the work is performed. Developing a reliable method for discovering and removing the factory's constraints improves the improvement process.

Douglas Engelbart distinguished these three levels in Toward High-Performance Organizations. The third level matters here because a better experimental method may help a team build several different capabilities. It also costs time. An elaborate improvement system with no worthwhile problem to work on has little to offer.

The work includes several purposes that are easy to lose sight of when delivery is urgent.

Discover
Find out what customers or colleagues need, and which problem is worth solving.
Deliver
Make a change that addresses that need.
Learn
Compare what happened with what you expected. Investigate the difference.
Develop
Turn a useful lesson into something people can use in later work.
Renew
Revisit the lesson when conditions change. Repair, replace or retire what no longer helps.

The connections matter more than the labels. An investigation might show that repeated release approvals compensate for a missing check. It might also show that the approval protects a decision the organization still needs a person to make. When the farm studies the factory follows these choices and asks whether the investigation method helps with a second problem. Improving our ability to improve follows these connections in more detail.

Let the evidence change the question

Learning changes our understanding of the software, its users and the way work is organized. It can change the outcome we pursue, the constraint we diagnose or the design we choose. It also develops the judgment needed to recognize an unfamiliar case. Generating a summary establishes none of those changes by itself.

Suppose every release waits for a senior engineer's approval. Preparing the approval material faster is one possible improvement. Finding that the rule was introduced for a release mechanism retired two years ago could suggest another. Before changing it, establish what the approval still protects and who has authority to revise it.

Argyris's double-loop learning questions the governing assumptions as well as the actions taken within them. That is how an improvement effort can discover it is solving the wrong problem.

Make the investigation capable of changing your mind. If slow test setup is the suspected cause, predict what will happen when a prepared case is available. Then watch the next eligible change. If it still waits for a product decision, the better fixture has not resolved the delay. This use of a prediction, observation and revised explanation follows the Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle.

Look at successful work too. An engineer who notices a growing database queue and pauses a migration has done something worth understanding. Ask what they saw, which options were available and how they decided. A quiet release does not tell you whether the system was forgiving or someone worked hard to keep it safe. Learning from things that go right explains how to investigate without turning one success into a universal procedure.

Make room for people to develop that understanding as execution becomes more automated. Ask them to predict a result, explain a surprise, change a condition and apply the lesson to unfamiliar work. Teach the missing concept when a tool's output cannot be interpreted. Shared judgment is part of the capability that the next investment can draw on.

Examine the learning method itself. Which questions exposed a mistaken assumption? Which comparison distinguished competing explanations? Could another investigator use that method to develop a new capability with less effort or a better result? This is a further opportunity for compounding, alongside reuse of the tool that the original investigation produced.

Leave room for a different future

Today's constraint is a good place to look for an improvement. It is a poor reason to assume today's work will always matter. A team can become excellent at maintaining an integration that a different product design would remove.

James March's exploration and exploitation model explains a difficulty: refining established knowledge can yield faster feedback than investigating unfamiliar possibilities. This framework therefore asks teams to consider three purposes for investment. Improving current work is one. Exploring a different approach is another. Preserving the ability to respond when familiar conditions fail is a third, proposed here as a practical addition.

Preservation might mean rehearsing a recovery without the usual expert, keeping a replacement route open, or protecting enough spare capacity to investigate a surprise. Each needs a reason and a cost. Keeping every old tool “just in case” makes adaptation harder too.

Revisit consequential knowledge when its assumptions change. A refund rule should say who owns it and what conditions it covers. A retrieved document can otherwise give an agent a convincing explanation for an obsolete decision. Getting better at the wrong thing explores this risk.

A capability has to reach the next person

Suppose a neighboring orders team shares a check for dangerous database locks with billing. Orders may understand its check perfectly while billing cannot use it. Copying the command does not transfer the background knowledge, access or judgment needed to interpret the result. Cohen and Levinthal's absorptive capacity explains why prior knowledge affects an organization's ability to recognize and apply new knowledge.

Give a receiver a real task and time to try it. Watch where they need help. Repair the example or teach the missing concept before adding another page of documentation. Count the receiving team's effort as part of the investment.

Some capabilities should stay local. Others benefit from temporary help between teams. A shared platform becomes worth considering when several teams have a sustained need and somebody can fund its operation. The adoption guide develops these choices. Success means the receiving teams can accomplish useful work with an acceptable support burden.

What AI changes

AI can help investigate unfamiliar behavior, compare designs, build the tools that recurring work needs and propose improvements to the engineering system. Its larger opportunity is to change which workflows are economical to automate and which questions are economical to investigate.

Use ordinary programs for predictable scheduling, provisioning, permission enforcement and cleanup. Use agents where interpretation or adaptation is useful. Then reconsider the surrounding work: who must start it, what it needs to know, how it finishes and which exceptions come back to a person. A more capable model does not remove those design decisions.

When generating alternatives becomes cheaper, the ability to choose between them deserves more attention. Ten plausible explanations still need an observation that distinguishes them. A hundred generated customer scenarios still need contact with real customers. A fast agent still needs a trustworthy way to discover that its change is wrong.

Invest in the evidence and understanding that let people make those decisions: reviewed domain cases, independent checks, accessible source material and clear authority to act. Watch the cost of interpreting results as well as producing them. An agent that starts changes faster than the team can verify them may simply create a larger queue. The economics of feedback examines where a shorter, more informative loop can help.

What would count as evidence?

The evidence guide distinguishes research findings, published accounts and the hypotheses we are pursuing. The open questions describe observations that would help settle them.

A useful case starts with an outcome and follows the work that produces it. Compare time to that outcome, human attention, intervention burden and total economic cost at the required quality. Keep elapsed waiting separate from effort. Include compute, failed attempts, supervision, maintenance and work shifted to another team.

For learning, record which explanation, assumption or decision changed and whether others can apply the understanding. For transformation, record which recurring work or dependency disappeared and what the organization can now accomplish. For compounding, record what a later investment inherited, what had to be adapted or built, and whether that inheritance reduced cost or improved the result.

For progress toward the tenfold ambition, return to valuable outcomes across the whole workflow. Local improvements, better understanding and new capabilities contribute only if they help that larger result. Do not multiply gains from separate tasks into a claim about overall effectiveness. Follow the connections and costs over successive investments.

A demonstration establishes behavior in its example. A field observation shows what happened to one team. A credible comparison helps distinguish the effect of the investment from practice, easier tasks or improved tools. Following several investments over time is necessary to investigate whether the ability to develop capabilities keeps improving.

DORA's 2025 research and AI Capabilities Model provide empirical context for organizational conditions associated with better AI outcomes. They do not establish this framework's compounding mechanism or a tenfold improvement. The measurement essay and proposed second-capability pilot separate those questions.

Also ask what has become feasible. Can the team now investigate replacing the billing service within an acceptable budget and risk? Can it evaluate a product opportunity it previously had to dismiss? What becomes possible? treats this expanding range of work as a strategic outcome, with the cost and quality conditions kept visible.

Start transforming one workflow

Choose a useful outcome and follow the work that produces it. Find the constraint, question the tasks and dependencies around it, and choose one bounded redesign. Say what you expect to happen and what would make you change course. Compare the result with the prediction, revise your understanding and keep what helps subsequent work.

The capability investment worksheet fits that conversation into a note you can paste into an issue. If the best result is a clearer decision or one less tool to maintain, record that. The next task will tell you more than a count of what you kept.

The idea

The workflow itself
is a design choice.

A team can generate a patch in minutes and still wait days for an environment, a decision or another team's release. What would have to change for that wait to disappear?

Transformation is the route we are exploring toward much greater effectiveness. Learning helps us choose and revise the changes; compounding lets earlier capabilities support later advances. Examine the tasks, team boundaries and assumptions that require someone to coordinate every step.

A recurring investigation can become a continuously available capability. A shared environment can become infrastructure created for each experiment. A change to an interface can remove a coordination meeting.

Consider three possible changes to a hypothetical team's environment setup. Each can help, but they change the work in different ways.

Acceleration

Complete the task faster.

An agent helps an engineer prepare an environment. The same request, manual start and handoff remain, but each takes less effort.

Automation

Let the procedure run.

A service provisions and retires an environment when eligible work arrives. Engineers no longer need to perform the setup each time.

Transformation

Organize the work differently.

Investigations create their own isolated environments. Teams can compare alternatives independently, and the environment request queue disappears from that workflow.

*

These changes can reinforce each other. The result to measure is useful work completed, including human attention, failures and upkeep. Automating an unnecessary procedure still leaves an unnecessary procedure. Question what the work is for.

The ambition

Make 10×
more achievable.

Build an engineering system that becomes progressively better at delivering value, learning and improving itself. Each advance should help us deliver a useful outcome or make the next advance easier.

Define effectiveness in terms of valuable outcomes, the total cost and human attention required, and the quality that must hold. Follow the work from a real need to an observed result. Discovery, decisions, architecture, implementation, verification, release and operation are all open to redesign.

Eliyahu Goldratt’s Theory of Constraints helps us choose where to invest. Name the outcome you want. Find what limits it. A faster step is not necessarily a faster result.

  1. Identify: follow recent changes and find what actually holds them up.
  2. Exploit: make better use of existing capacity before building more tools.
  3. Subordinate: direct the rest of the work toward finishing valuable changes, not starting more.
  4. Elevate: if the constraint remains, try the smallest investment that can change it.
  5. Repeat: check the outcome, the costs and what limits the work now.

If we designed this workflow from scratch today, knowing what AI can do, what would we still organize the same way? Tickets, handoffs, reviews, team boundaries and operational procedures are design choices, not fixed requirements.

At the constraint, ask what work can disappear, which dependency can be removed and what remaining procedure can run automatically. A simpler interface may remove a handoff. Clear decision ownership may settle an ambiguity. A diagnostic capability may resolve a familiar failure without waking its original author.

Automate as much of the remaining work as can meet its purpose at a worthwhile total cost. Use ordinary programs for predictable operations and agents where investigation or adaptation helps. Correctness, reliability and accountability remain requirements for the redesigned system.

A route toward 10× may emerge through a sequence of changes: learning reveals the next constraint, transformation removes it, and retained capabilities make a further change possible. Revisit work that was previously too expensive or uncertain to attempt. The task is to develop and test that route, including changes that fail.

Tenfold effectiveness is the ambition we are pursuing. Whether this approach can achieve it remains open. Count review, rework, compute and upkeep; improvements in separate tasks cannot simply be multiplied into a team-wide gain.

Different ways to organize engineering

Software farms.
Software factories.

Investigate and cultivate

A software farm starts with a question.

Does this design still fit the workload? Why does every release need the same intervention? Could we remove an obsolete component? A farm organizes continuing investigation and turns useful findings into evidence, understanding and opportunities for change.

An investigation can succeed by establishing why the existing design should stay. It need not produce a patch.

Implement and deliver

A software factory starts with work to do.

A factory organizes the route from sufficiently clear intent to an implemented, verified and delivered change. Environments, tools, evidence and release policy allow suitable classes of work to proceed with less routine coordination.

Its operating results and exceptions reveal where the machinery needs improving and which decisions still need human attention.

The same people and tools can serve both roles. A farm can investigate the factory itself; delivery and operations can supply its next questions. The learning should change shared understanding, decisions and capabilities, giving subsequent work a better starting point. People remain responsible for outcomes, priorities and delegated authority.

A self-improving engineering system

Let the farm investigate the factory.

When delivery repeatedly needs intervention, investigate why. A farm can test explanations and alternatives; the factory can implement a selected redesign within its authority. Observe whether the recurring intervention disappears. Reuse the new knowledge and machinery to address the next constraint.

How learning and capability compound

Make one advance
enable the next.

The environment service is useful on its own. Using it can also teach the team which dependencies an experiment really needs. That understanding may support a simpler boundary, while the provisioning tools make a new investigation cheaper to build. Together, they may let more delivery work proceed independently.

The tools, knowledge, architecture and ways of working that support this are engineering capital. Keeping them is accumulation; benefiting from them again is a return. Compounding is the further claim that they make new capabilities cheaper or more effective to develop, after adaptation and upkeep.

Learning connects these forms of capital: an engineer's understanding can become a useful tool, an experiment can justify a simpler design, and another engineer can develop judgment by working through the evidence. Keep the reasoning and its limits usable, and revise it when conditions change.

Reinvest some recovered capacity where the next constraint matters. Look for both effects: better outcomes now, and a stronger ability to develop the next capability. That is how compounding could support an ambitious trajectory over time.

Learning guides the transformation

Let experience change
how we work.

What did we expect, what happened, and what should change in our understanding or our next decision? A redesigned workflow gives us a way to investigate those questions.

Suppose the environment service removes setup waits, yet product experiments still stall. Perhaps nobody agreed which decision an experiment should inform. Learning changes the diagnosis and the next investment. Another provisioning tool would miss the lesson.

  1. Predict: explain why a change should improve the outcome and what would challenge that explanation.
  2. Observe: examine outcomes, costs and surprises. Investigate successful work as well as failures.
  3. Revise: compare explanations and reconsider the assumptions, decisions or workflow behind the result.
  4. Develop: turn validated learning into judgment, useful tools, clear rationale or better boundaries. Help others apply it.
  5. Build on it: try the capability in later work, renew what has become outdated and choose the next investment.

Human understanding matters throughout. Ask engineers to predict unfamiliar behavior, explain a surprise and use what they learned in a different case. Automation should leave the team better able to direct, judge and improve the system.

The investigation method can improve too. Earlier questions, rejected explanations and comparison methods may help the next investigator choose a better experiment. Check whether they actually make a later capability cheaper or more effective to develop. A stored lesson is only a starting point.

What these examples establish

The browser demo covers sequential duplicate delivery in memory. The separate Python walkthrough adds simulated restarts and interruptions at named points in temporary local storage. Neither establishes behavior under concurrent workers, machine failure or a real payment provider’s contract. Reusing the cases demonstrates a connection between capabilities; it does not measure whether the investment saves a team time.

Principles for transformation

Engineer the way
engineering works.

Use the next constraint to choose what should change.

Question the workflow.

Ask which tasks and handoffs still serve a purpose. Remove unnecessary work, redesign avoidable dependencies and automate the remaining procedures where the result justifies the cost.

Getting better at the wrong thing
Know why the check is green.

A passing test is useful when you know what it establishes. Ask what could still be wrong. The author and the reviewer can share a blind spot; so can a model and the tests it wrote.

Knowing what constitutes convincing evidence
Keep learning in the work.

Predict what will happen, try it, and explain the surprise. Let the evidence change a decision or assumption. Give people practice applying the lesson to a new case, and keep the tools and rationale that help them do it. Revisit what the lesson depends on when conditions change.

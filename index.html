Compounding Engineering

What would ten times
more effective
look like?

Our ambition is to become 10× more effective at delivering valuable software outcomes with AI.

A team can prepare a patch in minutes and still wait days to put it into use. Faster coding helps, but leaves us with a larger question: how much of the surrounding work could we remove, reorganize or let run without us?

These publications explore practical answers. We want each improvement to help with the next one, while people become better at understanding and directing the work.

Start with the work

Does this still need
to be an engineering task?

A customer asks to change their opening hours. Support raises a ticket, an engineer changes a configuration, and somebody reviews it.

An agent could prepare the change. A workflow could handle the whole request. Or the product could let the customer change their own hours, with the right permissions and checks.

These are different investments. The last removes a reason for the engineering queue to exist. It also creates a product to maintain and asks the customer to do something. Both belong in the comparison.

Acceleration

Prepare the change faster.

The request follows the same route, with less effort at one step.

Automation

Handle the request automatically.

The routine procedure runs within agreed rules. Someone deals with the exceptions.

Transformation

Change how the need is met.

The customer can do it themselves. This class of request leaves engineering altogether.

Six mechanisms to investigate

Where could
10× come from?

No single tool gives us the answer. These are six ways the work could change, and questions worth asking about each.

Elimination

Which requests, handoffs or procedures could stop being necessary?

Autonomy

Which kinds of work could finish without someone starting, checking or rescuing every instance?

Parallelism

Which dependencies could we remove so investigations and changes can proceed independently?

Simplification

What could we change about the software or organization so it needs less effort to understand and maintain?

Learning

How could cheaper investigation help people understand more and make better decisions?

Compounding

What could this investment leave behind that makes a different capability easier to develop?

The interactions matter. Understanding a recurring customer need might let us simplify a product boundary; that boundary might make independent verification and autonomous delivery practical. The essay follows these connections through a scheduling product.

Two jobs for an engineering organization

Software farms
and software factories

A farm starts with a question.

Why does every release need the same intervention? Do customers still use this feature? Could we remove a service? A software farm organizes continuing investigation into questions like these.

A finding might justify a change, expose a mistaken assumption or give us a reason to leave things alone.

Read about software farms

A factory starts with work to do.

A software factory takes a sufficiently understood need through implementation, verification, release and follow-up. Suitable work proceeds within agreed authority, with people handling decisions the system cannot make.

Recurring interruptions tell us where the arrangement still depends on somebody filling a gap.

These are roles, not necessarily departments. The same team can do both. The interesting next step is letting the farm study the factory: an investigation might improve a delivery procedure, or establish that the procedure should disappear. Follow that argument.

Follow the second improvement

What did the
earlier work make easier?

Suppose a team replaces a shared test environment with isolated environments. Setup gets easier. Later, the team uses the same machinery to compare two storage designs under the same workload. Building that comparison now takes less work.

The first result is a useful return. The second is the connection we mean by compounding: an earlier capability helps develop a new one. To judge it, include what had to be adapted and what it costs to keep both working.

The foundations explain how learning, engineering capital and the Theory of Constraints fit together. The worked transformation follows a customer integration team through several such choices, including a design it should abandon.

Can people still explain the system?

After delegating a change, ask a colleague to predict an unfamiliar failure and explain what evidence would settle it. A completed task and a stronger engineer are two different results.

Could the implementation be replaced?

Understanding the behavior we need can outlast a particular component. Whether replacing it is worthwhile depends on migration, evidence and operation as much as on writing its successor.

An ambition
to investigate

We have not established a general route to 10×. The site brings together research, published accounts and proposals for changing how engineering works. Illustrative examples help examine a design; they do not establish its returns.

The evidence guide explains what the sources support. Our open questions name observations that would support or weaken the ideas. Useful reports include failed attempts and changes that cost more than they saved.
